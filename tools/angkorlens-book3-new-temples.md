## Notes per temple

### 1. Prasat Ta Dong (ប្រាសាទតាដុង)
- Brick tower in dry forest and bamboo; grass and small trees growing on top.
- False door of brick panels; lintel carved directly into the brick (leaf scrolls, small figures).
- One side supported by metal scaffolding.
- Octagonal sandstone colonnettes and broken pieces lying around.
- Nearby rock shelter with offerings and portrait of a revered monk.
- Also called Prasat Tadong / Ta Ong. Location: side of Phnom Kulen, Chop Leu village, Svay Leu commune/district, Siem Reap (helloangkor.com).
- Originally 3 brick towers; 1 remains, faces east; false upper levels; outer brick wall with east gopura; well; sandstone remains; ruined laterite stair; hermit caves nearby.
- Recorded by Aymonier 1900, Lajonquière 1902 (IK 226), Parmentier 1913 ("Prasat Ta Don").
- Dating: lintels "reminiscent of 7th-century style" (helloangkor); 9th c. / Jayavarman II / Kulen style per a Facebook post — verify before the book.
- Still to confirm: deity, monk's name in shrine.

### 2. Spean Ta Ong (ស្ពានតាអុង) — also Spean Ta Oung / Ta Oak
- Location: Chong Spean village, Khvav commune, Chi Kraeng district, Siem Reap (APSARA, helloangkor).
- On the ancient East Royal Road: Angkor – Beng Mealea – Preah Khan Kampong Svay; crosses the Ta Ong stream; still in daily use.
- APSARA: 3rd-longest Angkorian bridge after Spean Toap and Kampong Kdei (same article also says "longest of 23" on the road — conflicting).
- Size: 63 m × 12 m × 7.3 m (helloangkor) vs 77 m between abutments, 13 m wide (Lajonquière 1902). 14 corbelled openings.
- Laterite with sandstone naga balustrades; naga-head fans among the finest.
- Date: between Suryavarman I and Suryavarman II, likely II; possible Jayavarman VII additions (helloangkor).
- APSARA repairs 2021 and 2022.

### 3. Tonle Snguot Temple (ប្រាសាទទន្លេស្ងួត)
- North of Angkor Thom; Nokor Krau village, Sangkat Kouk Chak, Siem Reap city.
- Hospital chapel (arogyasala) of Jayavarman VII, one of 102; one of four around Angkor Thom. Late 12th–early 13th c., Bayon style (APSARA).
- Sandstone tower with entrance hall, laterite enclosure, east gopura, library traces, NE basin; faces east to a reservoir (~800 × 400 m).
- Finds: stele K. 955 (Sanskrit, Saka 1108), K. 1112 graffiti, new 55-line Sanskrit stele (2020), dvarapala, ceramics, Medicine Buddha (now Preah Norodom Sihanouk-Angkor Museum).
- APSARA restoration 2019–2020; dam/reservoir works 2025 (NZ-supported Angkor Water Resilience).
- Photos show new grey replacement stones beside old ones; devatas; offerings inside.

### 4. Thma Dap Temple (ប្រាសាទថ្មដាប់)
- Anlong Thom village, Khnang Phnum commune, Svay Leu district, Siem Reap; Phnom Kulen group (helloangkor).
- Single brick sanctuary; carved brick pilasters, friezes, lintel, false floors; original stucco partly preserved.
- East: laterite steps, remains of outer enclosure. Surveyed 1973, 1979.
- Photos: crack down the front, wooden support frame, vegetation on top; nearby stream + waterfall.
- Age: 1,200+ years (APSARA, 2 Jan 2026); early Angkorian Kulen period, 9th c., Jayavarman II era (user info; no inscription).
- APSARA restoration from Jan 2026, 4 months: cracks E & W, sandstone doorway/lintel/pillars, vegetation removed. First structural restoration ever.
- Photos taken before restoration.
- Still to confirm: deity.

### 5. Bakong Temple (ប្រាសាទបាគង) — not in Books 1 & 2
- Roluos group, ancient capital Hariharalaya, Siem Reap.
- State temple of King Indravarman I, dedicated to Shiva in 881 CE; one of the first great Khmer temple-mountains.
- Sandstone pyramid of 5 levels; stone lions on stairways, elephants at corners of lower terraces.
- Two moats; causeway with 7-headed naga balustrades (among the earliest).
- Summit tower added later (12th c.). Restored by Maurice Glaize in the 1930s by anastylosis.
- Photos: devatas on summit tower, carved lintel framing the tower, moat with palms.
- Source: Wikipedia — Bakong.

### 6. Banteay Srei (ប្រាសាទបន្ទាយស្រី) — not in Books 1 & 2
- North of the main Angkor group; "citadel of women / of beauty".
- Foundation inscription: 967 CE, reign of Rajendravarman II; built by Brahmin advisor Yajnavaraha and brother Vishnukumara; dedicated to Shiva.
- Pink sandstone; three central towers; finest carving in Khmer art.
- Pediments: Vali vs Sugriva with Rama (Ramayana); Indra on 3-headed elephant sending rain on the burning Khandava forest (Mahabharata); Narasimha killing Hiranyakashipu.
- Rediscovered 1914; André Malraux tried to remove devatas 1923; Henri Marchal anastylosis from 1931.
- Sources: Sahapedia; Wikipedia — Banteay Srei.

### 7. Spean Toap (ស្ពានទ័ព) — also Spean Tip / O'Chik Bridge
- Cheung Tien village & commune, Chong Kal district, Oddar Meanchey; over the Sreng River.
- Longest ancient bridge of the Khmer Empire (helloangkor); ~150 m long, 14.5–16.4 m wide, ~10 m high, 28 openings (ron ta dev).
- Jayavarman VII, late 12th–early 13th c. (The Star / Ministry of Culture).
- On the NW Royal Road Angkor–Phimai; near Spean Memay and Spean Tbong.
- Laterite; sandstone naga balustrades and end caps.
- Ministry of Culture conservation since 2015 (8 phases). Bombed at both ends 15 Dec 2025 during border fighting; 68 balustrade sections damaged; inspected 12 Sep 2026.
- Photos taken during conservation (crane, bamboo scaffolding), before the damage.

### 8. Prasat Prohm Kel (ប្រាសាទព្រហ្មកិល)
- Cheung Tien commune, Chong Kal district, Oddar Meanchey; ~700 m north of Spean Toap; ~35 km from Samraong (helloangkor).
- Late 12th–early 13th c., Jayavarman VII, Bayon style.
- Laterite with sandstone door frames/windows; single tower facing east; windows on south side only, none on north.
- "House of fire" (dharmasala / vahni-griha) on the NW Royal Road Angkor–Phimai. Two basins nearby.
- Recorded: Lajonquière 1911 (IK 721); MH 580 (1926): "laterite building, basins and statues".
- Photos: stepped laterite tower reflected in pond; sandstone pieces lined at base; info sign board.
- Never restored (confirmed by Samnang, visited in person).
- Still to confirm: village name (helloangkor gives Cheung Tien and Srae Prang).

### 9. Spean Preah Chang'er (ស្ពានព្រះចង្អេរ) — also Spean Prah Changer
- Kôl village & commune, Angkor Chum district, Siem Reap (helloangkor).
- Laterite; 47 m long, 9 m wide; at least 12 corbelled openings; 12th–13th c. (no king named in source).
- NW Royal Road Angkor–Phimai; Kdei Ta Kom group.

### 16. Prasat Suor Prat N1 (ប្រាសាទ សួព្រ័ត្រខាងជើង) — North Tower 1 of 12
- Angkor Thom, east side of the Royal Square, ~180 m east of the Royal Palace terraces, in front of the North and South Khleangs. Twelve laterite/sandstone towers run north-south in two groups of 6, split by Victory Avenue; this submission is specifically the North 1 (N1) tower (only 2 photos sent).
- Khmer name used by Samnang: ប្រាសាទ សួព្រ័ត្រខាងជើង — matches helloangkor's "other names" field (ប្រាសាទ សួរព្រ័ត្រខាងជើង / "North Sour Prat"); minor romanization-only variants exist across sources (Suor/Sour/Suor Proat), not a real spelling conflict.
- Date CONFLICT (kept off the web page): earlier scholarship (and most popular sources, e.g. Wikipedia, renown-travel, theangkorguide) dates the towers to the late 12th/early 13th c., reign of Jayavarman VII, Bayon style. A 2005 archaeometric study (Uchida et al., cited by helloangkor) used stone sourcing, block size/technique, and radiocarbon dating of charcoal to propose an earlier date, late 11th to mid-12th c. (Angkor Wat period) — helloangkor states this is not settled. No inscription gives a foundation date or names a builder; earlier Jayavarman VII / Indravarman II attributions are not confirmed. The web page uses the safe range "11th-13th Century."
- Purpose: the name "Suor Prat" ("towers of the rope dancers") is a modern/folk name reflecting a local legend of tightrope performances between the towers at royal festivals — helloangkor explicitly notes there is no structural or archaeological evidence for this. Zhou Daguan (13th-c. Chinese envoy) recorded the towers may have been used to adjudicate disputes (the accused confined inside; the one who fell ill was deemed guilty) — a theory repeated across many tourist sites (penang-traveltips, canbypublications, tourismcambodia). Another theory (renown-travel, lonelyplanet) is that they served as reception stands for viewing festivals/parades on the plaza. No single theory is confirmed; page presents both briefly without picking a winner.
- Architecture (helloangkor): blocky towers with false diminishing levels for height, balustered side windows, a mandapa extending each entrance; built mainly of laterite with sandstone door/window frames and frontons. helloangkor notes the towers were never finished — frontons underdeveloped, no final rendering, built in haste. Entrances were walled up at the base, likely when the fronting terraces were added later.
- N1 restoration (the specific tower in these photos): found severely deteriorated with a large structural inclination; selected by the Japan-APSARA Safeguarding Angkor team (JASA/JSA, led by Waseda University) for a conservation project that fully dismantled the upper structure AND the foundation to study the masonry and the cause of the tilt before rebuilding with new techniques (geo-textile in the compacted foundation soil). Restoration/research work traced to ~2002 (UNESCO/Japan program), published as an academic paper in 2009 (Akazawa et al., AIJ Journal of Technology and Design). Separately, JSA's broader Royal Plaza work ran 1994-2005 and restored 2 of the 12 towers by 2005.
- Excavation history: cleared by Commaille in 1908; one tower restored by EFEO in the 1950s; JSA studied/restored 2 towers 1994-2005 (N1 among the identified restoration subjects per the academic paper title). No inscriptions found anywhere at the 12-tower complex (helloangkor, confirmed).
- Records: 1926 Liste des Monuments Historiques #373 ("Prasat Suor Prat — Twelve laterite towers; statues, lingas"). IK 480.04; CISARK 1150; helloangkor Ref HA12261.
- Photos (2 only — Samnang may add more later): cover = close frontal view of the tower in full sun (polished, slight warm-cast correction). Second photo = wider shaded view showing the tower among trees with a neighboring tower visible behind (polished, no colour correction needed).
- Still to confirm: precise date (12th-13th c. vs. 11th-12th c., per conflict above), whether this is definitely the JASA-restored N1 tower or a different north tower, any linga/yoni finds specific to N1, exact 1908/1950s/1994-2005 restoration scope for N1 vs. the complex as a whole.

### 18. Phnom Da Temple (ប្រាសាទភ្នំដា) — first temple outside Siem Reap/Angkor Park
- IMPORTANT: this is the first AngkorLens Book 3 submission from outside the Siem Reap/Angkor Archaeological Park area. Phnom Da sits atop a hill in the ancient Angkor Borei area, Praek Da village, Kouk Thlok commune, Angkor Borei district, Ta Keo (Takeo) province, in far southern Cambodia near the Vietnamese border region — not part of the Angkor Park proper, so note this for the book's geographic framing/section if Book 3 is organized by area.
- Khmer name used by Samnang: ប្រាសាទភ្នំដា — helloangkor's own site actually shows a garbled/typo'd Khmer string (បា្រសាទភ្នំដា, a character-order error) across several of its Phnom Da-area pages; the standard, correct spelling Samnang uses is what the page shows. Not a real conflict, just a note that the source site has a recurring typo.
- Date CONFLICT (kept off the web page): most tourism/reference sources (helloangkor, Lonely Planet, Canby Publications, Via Michelin) date the standing brick-and-laterite temple to the 11th century, with helloangkor noting it may replace an earlier structure. Wikipedia's "Angkor Borei and Phnom Da" article instead states the oldest standing Khmer stone temple on the hill may date to the 6th century CE, and separately links sculptures found at the site to King Rudravarman's reign (514-539 CE) — though one cited scholar (Stephen Murphy) places the Vishnu imagery as late as the 7th-8th century, and another source (Nancy Dowling) suggests the conventional statue dating may be inaccurate. It is not clear from available sources whether the "6th-century stone temple" Wikipedia describes is the same standing brick/laterite structure most other sources date to the 11th century, or a separate/earlier structure on the same hill. The web page uses the 11th-century date (majority of direct-visit tourism sources) and does not mention the 6th-century claim or Rudravarman.
- Architecture: brick superstructure on an almost-entirely-laterite base; sandstone doorframe and carved octagonal columns at the entrance. Unusual feature: the true opening faces NORTH (most Khmer temples face east), with false doors in stone on the other three sides (helloangkor). Elaborate stonework includes large pediments and a double-headed naga forming an arch over the entrance.
- Inscriptions (5 recorded): K.549, K.556, K.670 (in two sections), K.830 (dated Saka 1028), and K.1216/Ka 29 (Sanskrit, pre-Angkorian, no clear date). helloangkor does not give inscription content/translations beyond the reference numbers.
- Famous sculptures: the Angkor Borei/Phnom Da area has yielded major pre-Angkorian and early-Angkorian sculpture — Harihara, standard Vishnu, an eight-armed Vishnu (6 of 8 attributes extant, 2 lost, each linked to one of the 8 Lokapala guardian deities per Wikipedia), Rama, Krishna lifting Mount Govardhana (Govardhanadhara), Balarama, and a meditating Buddha; many photographed by EFEO 1935-1960. Most finds are now in the National Museum in Phnom Penh. The best-known single piece is the "Triad of Phnom Da," a schist Vishnu-Rama-Balarama group (Wikipedia); not currently on site.
- Status: UNESCO World Heritage Tentative List entry "Angkor Borei and Phnom Da" since 1 September 1992 (Cultural category), submission renewed 27 March 2020 (Wikipedia) — not full World Heritage status, only tentative-list.
- Photos: user sent 4 photos. Photo 3 shows a small shrine/altar with offerings and a Nandi(-style) statue inside what appears to be a walled enclosure or cave on the hill — likely the "ancient cave" near the contemporary pagoda mentioned on helloangkor's separate "Wat Phnom Da" page, not necessarily inside the main 11th-century temple itself; kept as submitted, labeled generically, exact identification not confirmed. Photo 4 is a close doorway/entrance detail showing active conservation scaffolding inside the opening (warm colour cast toned down).
- Still to confirm: which structure Wikipedia's "6th-century stone temple" refers to vs. the 11th-century brick temple in these photos (date conflict above — kept off the page entirely), exact identification/dedication of the cave-altar shrine in photo 3, builder/king (no source names one for the standing temple), current restoration project visible in photo 4 (no source found describing it).

### 19. Prasat Leak Neang (Chapel of the Hospital) (ប្រាសាទលាក់នាង)
- Note on naming confusion: there are multiple distinct sites called "Leak Neang" in the Angkor region (helloangkor lists several, with a warning that the name recurs confusingly). The one matching the user's description ("close to Ta Keo temple") is the "Chapel of the Hospital," located about 150m west of Ta Keo temple inside Angkor Thom — confirmed by multiple independent sources (helloangkor, penang-traveltips, tourismcambodia, justsiemreap, actionstour) describing it as west of Ta Keo, near Chau Say Tevoda/Thommanon and Spean Thma bridge. This is NOT the same as the 10th-century single-tower Leak Neang opposite Pre Rup (a different site, inscription K.265, Rajendravarman II era) or the Leak Neang near Phnom Bok (Banteay Srei district). Used the Ta Keo-adjacent Chapel of the Hospital identification throughout.
- Date: late 12th century (1186 AD per inscription), reign of King Jayavarman VII.
- Function: one of the four hospital chapels ("Arogyasala") that ringed Angkor Thom at each cardinal point — Tonle Sngout (north), Ta Prohm Kel (south), Tromoung (west), Leak Neang (east) — part of a network of 100+ hospitals Jayavarman VII founded empire-wide (cambodianess, helloangkor, lucasvarro).
- Inscription: a Sanskrit stele found at the site (now displayed in the Angkor National Museum) dated 1186 AD, with 28-30 lines of text per side across 4 sides, describing a royal decree on hospital administration and listing medicines/remedies (vetiver, vinegar, oils) — a key primary source for Angkorian-era healthcare (cambodianess "Picture of the Day" article).
- Architecture: sandstone, Bayon style, single central tower — in relatively good condition compared to its sister chapels (lucasvarro). Canbypublications notes it is of "very similar design" to Ta Prohm Kel near Angkor Wat, the other surviving hospital chapel.
- Photos: user sent 4 photos. Photos 1, 2 and 3 (as received) were near-identical full-tower views from essentially the same standing spot — per project rule, kept only one (the clearest, best-framed) as cover, cropped a second to isolate the carved guardian/devata figure in its niche beside the false door (distinct zoomed detail not visible as clearly in the cover), and dropped the third as a true duplicate with no unique content. Photo 4 (kept as-is) is a wider approach shot from the path showing the tower's full setting among trees.
- Still to confirm: precise identification of the carved figure at the base (bodhisattva vs. generic guardian/devata — sources don't specify), full content/translation of the 1186 inscription beyond the general "healthcare decree" summary, whether the chapel retains its original enclosure wall or library building (most hospital-chapel pages describe a wider Arogyasala complex of pond + enclosure + library + shrine, but the submitted photos show only the central tower).

### 20. Daun Troung Temple (Prasat Plang) (ប្រាសាទដូនទ្រុង)
- Location note: not inside the Angkor Archaeological Park — this is a Puok district site, Plang village, Prey Chruk commune, Siem Reap province, reached by a rough dirt track off National Road 6 before the river crossing.
- Other names (helloangkor): Prasat Plan, Prasat Plang, Plang (Pr.), Pr. Plang.
- Date: no construction date given for the sanctuary itself. The in-situ lintel inscription K.452 is dated Saka 910 (~988 CE), reign of King Jayavarman V — but that only dates the inscription/dedication, not necessarily the original construction of the structure it is set into (Coedès notes the inscribed piece may be a reused older pillar).
- Inscription K.452: Sanskrit text, 12 lines forming six stanzas (five çloka, one mālinī), upper portion missing/lost. Records an unnamed individual who erected an image of Lokeçvara (a form of Avalokiteshvara) and endowed a well-provisioned monastery. Source: Coedès, Inscriptions du Cambodge, vol. 5, p. 156 (1953), via helloangkor.
- Architecture: square sandstone sanctuary, short east-facing hall, laterite "library" annex in the southeast, laterite enclosure wall, entrance gopura, large basin to the east. helloangkor notes the overall plan resembles the many hospital chapels (Arogyasala) built under Jayavarman VII, but explicitly states no "hospital stele" has been found at this site — so it is NOT classified as a confirmed hospital chapel, just architecturally similar. Kept this nuance off the public page to avoid implying a confirmed hospital-chapel identification.
- Records: Lunet de Lajonquière 1911 (Inventaire III, p.315, listed as Prasat Plang, Srok Puok, Khet Siemreap). 1926 Liste des Monuments Historiques #527: "sandstone sanctuary with annexes and a reused inscribed pier." IK 654; MoCFA/EFEO (CISARK) 533; MH 527.
- Current condition: central tower visibly leaning, braced with substantial temporary wooden scaffolding in several submitted photos to prevent further collapse. Site heavily overgrown.
- Photos: user sent 10 photos — reviewed all for near-duplicates per project rule, but found none; each shows genuinely distinct content (4 close-up carving/pediment details — a figure riding a lion/garuda, a round medallion, a makara/naga scroll, and a lintel row of seated figures; 1 doorframe/lintel detail with foliage; 5 wide or structural views of the tower from different angles/sides, with and without the scaffolding visible, in different lighting). All 10 kept; cover chosen as the clearest wide shot showing the leaning tower and its bracing against blue sky.
- Still to confirm: original construction date of the sanctuary (distinct from the 988 CE inscription date), whether the reused/inscribed lintel piece Coedès mentions affects the building's dating, identity of the dedicator named incompletely in the damaged inscription, and whether this should be classified as a true Jayavarman VII-era hospital chapel despite the absence of a hospital stele (kept ambiguous on the public page — described only as "resembling" that layout).

### 24. Beng Mealea (Prasat Beng Mealea) (ប្រាសាទបឹងមាលា)
- No naming conflict: unlike several recent submissions, Beng Mealea is a single, world-famous, unambiguous site — it is not shared with any other place name in Cambodia. Double-checked it was genuinely not already in tools/temples.json or tools/new_temples.json (searched for "beng", "mealea", "moal" — no matches) despite being one of the best-known Angkorian temples, since it seemed surprising it had not been added yet.
- Location: Beng Mealea Village, Beng Mealea Commune, Svay Leu District, Siem Reap Province, about 40km east of the main Angkor temple group and ~67km from Siem Reap town by road, roughly midway along the ancient Royal Road between Angkor and Preah Khan of Kampong Svay.
- Date: no foundation stele has ever been found, so the date is inferred from architectural style alone (nearly identical to Angkor Wat). Traditionally attributed to King Suryavarman II (1113–1150 AD), built around the same time as Angkor Wat; a newer inscription has led some scholars to instead credit completion to King Tribhuvanadityavarman (r. 1149 – c.1180 AD), a Buddhist king who succeeded Suryavarman II. Both datings are given on the public page since this is a genuinely live scholarly question, not a settled fact.
- Description: a large, flat-plan (non-pyramidal) Hindu temple dedicated primarily to Vishnu, often called a "two-dimensional version of Angkor Wat" for its closely similar layout. Built chiefly of sandstone with laterite. Surrounded by a large moat (~1.2km x 0.9km). Some carvings show Buddhist motifs. Jayavarman VII later added further structures around the site, including Kansaeng Temple. Nearby related sites recorded by helloangkor include Don Chan Temple, Ta Phou Temple, Chrei Temple (Beng Mealea), and Kong Pluk Temple — not covered in this entry since the user's photos show only the main temple.
- Historical records: 1926 Liste des Monuments Historiques, #301. Reference ID HA11594 (helloangkor). Recent APSARA National Authority excavation work on the eastern causeway (2024) and western causeway (2025).
