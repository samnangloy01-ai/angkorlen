Kandork Yeak Temple (Preah Pithu Y)

Kandork Yeak Temple — ប្រាសាទកណ្តកយក្ស (Preah Pithu Y; also written "Temple y")

Location: Angkor Thom, Sangkat Nokor Thum, Siem Reap. Kandork Yeak is the fifth temple of the Preah Pithu group (T, U, V, X and Y), a group that was probably not planned as one. Wikipedia places it north of Temple V, and Hello Angkor says it differs greatly from the other four.

Hello Angkor dates the temple to the 12th century and says it was originally dedicated to Vishnu. APSARA's page on the Preah Pithu group gives the beginning of the 12th century and lists the reign of Suryavarman II for the group as a whole. Dating specifically for Temple Y varies: Wikipedia places its style between Angkor Wat and the Bayon.

What to see: the temple is built of sandstone on a large rise. Unlike the small cruciform plan of the other four temples, this is an elongated building: a front room joins a longer room through a short hall, which also gives the north and south entrances. Wikipedia adds that it has no basement or stairways, that it stands on an earthen platform, that the larger chamber has mostly collapsed, and that the sanctuary chamber measures 3.5 m by 3.0 m and held a linga 0.95 m tall.

The carving is the reason to come. Two half pediments tell tales of Vishnu, and Wikipedia identifies the scenes as Krishna's defeat of the asura Bana and the three giant steps of Vamana. My photographs show crowded scenes with attendants and animals, a chariot wheel, small pediments over the doors, and pilasters decorated with roundels. A false door stands on the west side. Hello Angkor also records a small basin to the north with laterite edging, a terrace structure to the east, and an unidentified mound in the north-east corner holding a linga and pedestals.

Records: Aymonier mentioned the group in 1904 and Lajonquière listed Temple y in 1911. It carries IK 485 and CISARK 385, and the group is in the 1926 list of Historic Monuments (no. 374). Wikipedia says the Preah Pithu temples were first cleared by Jean Commaille in 1908 and then by Henri Marchal from 1918 to 1920.
